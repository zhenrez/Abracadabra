# "Peace be Upon You, Exalted Angels": on Hekhalot, Liturgy and Incantation Bowls

Published: 2016-01-01. 10 citations.

## Abstract

Any one working within the field of magic in Judaism in Late Antiquity and the early Middle Ages knows the difficulties besetting any attempt to define it.1 Despite these difficulties, which exist in Judaism just as they do in any religious culture, there are not very many cases of hesitation when one tries to identify magic texts in practice. Ancient Palestinian amulets are a genre apart from other types of writings, and the same applies to the incantation bowls which stem largely from Babylonia. In dealing with the material from the Cairo Geniza, it is in most cases clear that a given fragment was not intended to form part of the regular communal ritual, and as a consequence we may assign it with little hesitation to the domain of the private practice of magic. This applies also to the hundreds of fragments of magical handbooks, containing formulae of spells and instructions for practice, as well as to the large number of amulets preserved there. There are nevertheless cases of doubt, mostly with regard to the proper description of Geniza manuscripts. These difficulties are not merely technical, but are symptomatic of the more general problem of definition and delimitation of the field.

## FAQs

How does the Hekhalot literature influence Babylonian magic bowls? add

Research indicates that some Babylonian incantation bowls utilize language derived from the Hekhalot literature, suggesting a shared mystical-magical tradition. This indicates a significant cultural interplay, highlighting the origins and development of the Hekhalot texts within the magical practices of the time.

What key features link the magical spells to Hekhalot mysticism? add

The paper identifies numerous linguistic and thematic echoes between magical spells in incantation bowls and Hekhalot texts, such as the expression 'exalted angels'. Specific phrases, like 'great brightness', reaffirm this link, demonstrating a shared literary tradition.

What evidence supports the intersection of liturgy and magic in Jewish texts? add

The analysis reveals instances where traditional liturgical language is co-opted for magical applications, such as blessings invoking angels' peace. This merging reflects the historical interplay between religious practices and magical rituals in Jewish culture.

What historical context surrounds the use of Hekhalot language in magic? add

The study reveals that, during the medieval period, a harmony emerged among Hekhalot mysticism, liturgy, and magical texts. This situation likely reflects both a legitimization of magical practices and an adaptation of elevated religious language.

How have scholars debated the cultural origins of Hekhalot texts? add

Scholarly consensus previously leaned towards a Palestinian origin for Hekhalot texts, supported by their Hebrew composition. However, evidence of connections to Babylonian traditions and mystical practices challenges these assumptions, revealing a more complex cultural narrative.
